# 0016. Interest income, and the debt rule while the key rate is held

Status: accepted (September 2026), in part. **The owner's decision it records: a tax change acts first through the budget.** Setting income tax locks it and the debt rule steps aside, so a cut means less revenue, a deficit, rising debt and more household income and demand, and the central bank's reaction comes second (the request, below); the tax texts lead with the budget and debt. It answers the owner's request on tax levers and the review items ECON-2 to ECON-5 of the verification of phases 1–4. Part B, the debt rule while only the key rate is locked, is done: it supersedes the open questions R4 and the counter-cyclical term of [decision 0015](0015-downturn-escape-clause.md) and closes lever-vetting open item 22. Part A, the interest-income channel, was measured in both models and changed in neither; the long-run path of a held tax change with the central bank's rule acting (ECON-5, open item 21) is kept as it is: by the owner's decision of 30 September 2026 it is the lesson, not a defect (see the end). Part B was revised after its review (30 September 2026): the first version still let the debt term raise taxes in a slump and cut them in a boom while the key rate was held; now debt never moves the tax against the cycle.

## The request

The owner: "Why would changing income tax not directly affect debt? That is the most normal effect, not a central bank reaction. Taxes, debt and wage negotiations will be the most common levers the user wants to play with. Government debt is exactly NOT the locked element; it's the reaction."

So, in the default state (both padlocks open), setting income tax locks it, the debt rule steps aside, and the effects should be led by the budget: a cut means less revenue, a deficit, rising debt and more household income and demand; the central bank's reaction comes second and should steady the economy. The verification found instead:

- **ECON-5.** Iceland income tax −2.5 held while the key-rate rule acts: output +3.23%, the price level +13.1% and still accelerating, the key rate +4.8 points and government debt +47 points of GDP at month 240, flagged Explosive. The reference economy's tax rate −2 gave output +80% and a key rate above 60%, and its range had been cut to −2 to stay finite.
- **REF-KEYRATE-ALONE.** The reference key rate held at 2.25% with its debt rule acting was Explosive (the price level +19% and accelerating); 4.75% gave output −13.4%, and 0% a price level +59%.
- **ECON-2, ECON-3, ECON-4.** With only Iceland's key rate locked, the debt rule's escape clause read where the unused central-bank rule was heading, not the rate in force (the proposal's R4), and the rule leaned on debt alone, so it raised taxes in private-demand slumps.

## Part A: the interest-income channel, measured

**Where interest lands.** In Iceland the government's nominal bonds are held by banks (14.4% of GDP at baseline), pension funds (13.4, and all 19.8 of the indexed bonds), older households (4.0, a placeholder), non-residents (4.1) and the central bank (1.0). Banks pay depositors the key rate less a margin; pension funds credit their income to members' rights, which reach pensions only as they are paid out (about a fifth of pensioners' rights a year); households spend `aK` = 0.2 of real interest and dividends at once, and the rest joins their savings, of which they spend 10–15% a year above normal (`aW`); non-residents' interest stays in their króna deposits. In the reference economy the bank holds most of the bonds and pays out all its profit as dividends, and households spend 0.9 of disposable income, interest and dividends included, and 9% of their deposits a year.

**The evidence.** No study we cite estimates the marginal propensity to consume out of interest income as such. What the evidence does show is that the propensity is low for the households who receive most interest income: it falls with cash on hand (Jappelli and Pistaferri 2014; their 2010 survey), it is small for households that are not hand-to-mouth, those with ample liquid wealth (Kaplan and Violante 2022), and that heterogeneity is what drives the interest-rate exposure channel of monetary policy (Auclert 2019). Spending out of financial wealth is about 3 cents a króna a year (Poterba 2000; Chodorow-Reich, Nenov and Simsek 2021). Iceland's `aK` = 0.2 is an assumption consistent with that, and its bond holdings follow the data (`pfGovShare`), so Iceland's routing was already close to the evidence; its savings are spent faster than the wealth evidence says. The reference economy spends interest like wages, at 0.9, which is high for the households that receive it.

**Why none of them makes a held tax change settle.** Two things keep the runaway going after the household channel is cut:

1. **Debt compounds in a zero-growth economy.** With tax rates held, a lasting deficit adds interest to interest at the real bond rate less growth, about 3.5% a year here (a 3% neutral real rate, a 0.5-point spread, no growth). The lever report's Explosive test asks whether the last year's move is at least 1.2 times the move five years earlier, and e^(5 × 0.035) = 1.19: any rise in the key rate or in the deficit tips a held tax change's debt path over it. This is the Leeper (1991) result, active monetary policy with no fiscal anchor, made sharper by the zero-growth baseline; in Iceland's data nominal growth and bond yields are close (r − g near zero), and the model's balanced-growth baseline (roadmap v2) would bring that back.
2. **In Iceland, the króna.** The deficit sends krónur abroad, through imports and through the pension funds, which buy foreign assets as their income grows; non-residents then hold more krónur than they want, and portfolio balance weakens the króna, so exports rise and imported inflation keeps the key rate climbing, which raises the pension funds' income again. With the portfolio term switched off, inflation after the tax cut stays at about 0.3 point and the key rate 2 points up; with it, inflation climbs to 1.1 points. This is lever-vetting item 4 (booms weaken the real króna), which króna stage 2 is to fix.

So the evidence-based changes shrink the runaway but cannot stop it here, and each moves calibrated behaviour that rests on the old channel. Adopting them needs the joint recalibration the long-run-anchors proposal plans (the reference economy's R6 and interest in real terms; phase 5 for Iceland's wage block), not a change in passing. The texts now lead with the budget instead, and say where the long-run drift comes from.

## Part B: the debt rule while only the key rate is locked (decided)

While the key rate is locked the central bank does not react, so the debt rule must steady the economy in its place, leaning against the cycle with a slow debt term under it (Kirsanova, Leith and Wren-Lewis 2009), and the proposal asked for exactly that with R4. Both models now do it; while the key rate is unlocked nothing changes.

- **A counter-cyclical term.** Iceland: `phiGap` = 0.5 income-tax points per point of the output gap the central bank reads from the labour market (decision 0012); the reference economy: `fiscalCycle` = 0.5 points per 1% of output above capacity, the gap its Taylor rule reads. On tax bases of about two-thirds and 87% of GDP that is 0.3–0.45% of GDP of revenue per point of gap on top of the automatic stabilisers, the size of the discretionary responses estimated for OECD governments (Galí and Perotti 2003). At 1 Iceland's slumps were shallower still (tourism −60: output −3.4% at month 60 against −4.8%) at the cost of tax swings twice as large.
- **A slow debt term.** Iceland `phiTauHeld`, the reference `fiscalHeldShare`: the debt term at half its strength. At a quarter a held rate 3 points above neutral turned Iceland's output positive before month 480, which the proposal's acceptance test forbids; at half no held rate from +1 to +3 points does, and a model test gates it.
- **Debt never moves the tax against the cycle** (added after the review). In the first version the half-strength debt term still outweighed the counter-cyclical one: after tourism −60 it raised income tax from month 90, +4.5 points by month 240, with the output gap still −3 to −1%; with the key rate held at 0% it cut income tax 1.9 points by month 240 while output stayed about 1% above potential, as the lower interest bill and inflation shrank the debt ratio; the reference economy did the same (4.75% held: the tax rate +1.7 points with output 1.6% below capacity; 0%: −1.6 points with output 2% above it). Now, while the key rate is locked, a rise the debt term asks for fades out as output falls below potential and is gone once the gap is −1% (`heldSlumpBand`, `fiscalSlumpBand`), and from there the rule does not raise the tax at all, whatever rate is held; a cut the debt term asks for fades out as output rises above potential and is gone at +0.25% (`heldBoomBand`, `fiscalBoomBand`). Each is a `combine` on the rule's target with a regime label ("Key rate held: debt adds no tax while output is below potential", "…low debt cuts no tax while output is above potential"). The bands differ on purpose. With the key rate held, the debt rule is all that stops debt compounding (Leeper 1991), and a rate held above neutral brings a long, mild slump: with a quarter-point slump band the rule paid too little of the interest bill, and a 6% hold turned output positive by month 130, failing the acceptance test. Holding back a cut costs nothing but time: debt stays low, and the cut comes once the boom has passed.
- **R4, the escape clause on the rate in force (Iceland).** The first version read the held rate, and only in a slump; the guard above takes its place while the key rate is held, since it stops any debt-driven rise in a slump whatever rate is held, so the clause no longer reads the unused central-bank rule (ECON-2). While the key rate is unlocked the escape clause is unchanged.
- **The implied neutral rate stays a measure of the usual rules.** Iceland's switch `heldRateFiscal` (1) turns the held-rate form on; the lever report's implied-neutral scan (decision 0012), which holds the key rate to find the rate the economy needs, sets it to 0 (`ImpliedNeutralSpec.hold`), so holding the rate there does not change how the debt rule acts. The first version let the scan run the held-rate form, and the diagnostic reversed (tourism −60 "needed" 6.9% instead of 1.65%).

`taxRuleTarget` (Iceland) and `taxRuleTarget` (reference) read the key rate's padlock (`locks`), and their terms, regime labels and explanations say all of this. The key-rate lever's texts, the debt rules' stabiliser descriptions and the note for when only the key rate is locked (`lockedAloneNote`; since 2 October 2026 in the lever's info panel, [decision 0015](0015-downturn-escape-clause.md)) say that the debt rule steadies the economy in the central bank's place and that debt never pushes the tax against the cycle.

## What stays open, and the owner's choice

- **ECON-5 (open item 21): decided by the owner, 30 September 2026. It is the lesson, not a defect.** The owner's reply (30 September 2026): "Isn't that just the realism of it? If you run deficits that are faster than GDP growth, you accumulate debt to GDP over time, constantly expanding the money supply, reducing the ISK rate. If unlocked we can use the key rate to counteract, but if the key rate is locked, then it should develop according to theory." So a held tax change is financed by debt and nothing pays it back: a cut runs deficits that grow faster than the economy, debt piles up against GDP, money keeps expanding and the króna weakens, while the central bank's rule leans against the inflation (Iceland income tax −2.5: deficit 1.55% of GDP in month 1 with the key rate unmoved; at month 240 debt +47 points of GDP, broad money +39%, the króna 15% weaker, the key rate +4.8 points). The test that no moderate tax step is Explosive is replaced by one that pins this chain in both models (`tests/harness/lever-report.test.ts`), and the tripwire on its size stays. The income-tax lever's note says it while income tax is locked and the key rate is not (since 2 October 2026 in the lever's info panel, not at the top of the lever panel). A balanced-growth baseline (the start-from-today work, r − g near zero) will change the magnitudes, not the chain. What follows is the measurement behind the options the owner was offered. Held income tax with the central bank's rule acting runs away in both models, for the two reasons in part A. As the lever report defines Explosive (the last year's move at least 1.2 times the move five years earlier), that cannot be avoided in a zero-growth baseline: a lasting deficit compounds at r − g of about 3.5% a year, e^(5 × 0.035) ≈ 1.19, so any rise in the key rate or the deficit tips it over; Iceland's króna adds to it. Re-solving the reference baseline on households' spending out of deposits, so that interest can be spent at 0.25 without doubling government (the review's suggestion), shrinks the runaway (tax −1: output +6.1 → +2.9%) but does not stop it (table in part A). The owner decides among: (1) a balanced-growth baseline (roadmap v2), which brings r − g near zero, as in Iceland's data; (2) króna stage 2, which removes Iceland's drift through the króna (lever-vetting item 4) but not the compounding; (3) a fiscal anchor while income tax is held, for example the debt rule acting on another instrument (spending, or a second tax) so that something still pays a held tax change back. Only (1) and (3) act on the compounding itself, so only they can make the criterion reachable in both models. The texts lead with the budget and debt and say where the drift comes from.

- **A tax lever handed back while the key rate is held: it can stand still until the cycle has passed (recorded 2 October 2026; a question for the owner).** The owner's rule for the padlocks is that unlocking hands a lever back at once, to react with everything else. With the key rate unlocked, or locked at neutral, the debt rule does: it moves the tax in the very next month, about 4% of the way from the rate held toward where it is heading (`tests/models/unlock.test.ts`). With the key rate held away from neutral, part B's rule that the tax never moves against the cycle applies to a handed-back rate too. Where the rule is heading is then no lower than the rate in force while output is more than 0.25% above potential, and no higher while it is more than 1% below, so a rate the user held above the rule's own in a boom, or below it in a slump, is the rule's destination: the lever shows "auto" at that rate and stands still until the boom or slump has passed. Measured, months without a move after unlocking: Iceland, key rate 0%, income tax +1 held a year, 104 months (+2: 64; +0.5 with the key rate at 1%: 87); key rate 8%, income tax −1 held three years, 37 months; the reference economy, key rate 1%, tax rate +1 held a year, 33 months; key rate 6%, tax rate −1, 34 months. Held the other way (a tax below the rule's own in a boom, above it in a slump) the rule moves it at once. Letting the rule unwind a held shift as far as its cycle-consistent rate (in a boom the target no lower than the smaller of the rate in force and the counter-cyclical term's rate, the mirror in a slump) removes every standstill, but it cannot tell a held shift from the rule's own earlier lean, so it also lets the rule raise the tax as a slump eases and cut it as a boom fades: the three model tests of "no rise while output is more than 1% below potential, no cut while it is more than a quarter point above" then fail (Iceland tourism −60: a rise of up to 0.018 point a month in the slump; key rate 0%: a cut of up to 0.004 point a month in the boom; the reference economy: up to 0.008). Those tests are the owner's review of this decision, so part B is kept, and the standstill is pinned by a test and said in both tax levers' definitions. The owner decides whether a hand-back should be an exception; it would need a way to tell a held shift from the rule's own path (a variable for the part of the rate in force that the user set), and new numbers for this decision's tables.
- **A cost of part B, recorded (final repairs, review KEYLOCK-VAT-GAP).** With only the key rate locked, the held-rate debt rule leaves a VAT-driven output gap of about 1% open for twenty years: VAT +2.5, output −0.99% at month 60 and −0.98% at month 240 (−0.63% before this decision); VAT −2.5, +1.03% and +0.91%. The rule moves income tax only about 0.9 point (a cut of 0.93 at month 240 after the rise), because the gap it leans against is read from the labour market, where unemployment moves only 0.3 point, so the counter-cyclical term asks for a cut of about a quarter point, and the debt term is at half strength. The table above says "unchanged" for VAT +2.5 against this decision's first version; against the base before the decision the long run is worse, so "steadies the economy in the central bank's place" holds for private-demand shocks and less well for tax shocks. A larger `phiGap` would narrow it, but must be tested against the 480-month acceptance test (holds 1–3 points above neutral stay below baseline); it is left for the joint refit (lever-vetting open item 24).
