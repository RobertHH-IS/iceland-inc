# 0002. Porting the Iceland model from engine v1

Status: accepted, with the first platform version of the Iceland model (September 2026).

Engine v1 (`legacy/v1-engine`) was one hand-written monthly step of about 270 lines. The port declares the same economy as 14 modules in `src/models/iceland/`, and the kernel now does the accounting, payments, checks, scheduling, steady state and influences. This record lists the kernel and harness changes the port needed, how v1's constructs map onto the model language, and how the port's results compare with v1's.

## 2. Contract, kernel and harness changes

All are small and backwards compatible; the reference model's results are unchanged.

| # | Where | Change | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `RuleDef.adjust` (types.ts), compile.ts, machine.ts | Optional `form: 'exponential'`: the share of the gap closed each step is 1 − e^(−speed·dt) instead of speed·dt. No overshoot warning for this form. | v1 used this exact first-order lag everywhere (`kf`). With the linear form, speeds such as the króna's 12 a year close the whole gap in one month (v1: 63%), and every speed of 1.5–3 a year runs 7–15% fast. The exponential form reproduces v1 exactly and changes less when the step is halved. |
| 2 | `LegDef` doc (types.ts), compile.ts, format.ts | A `revalue` between two holders of the same instrument is a reclassification: value moves from `from` to `to`, no cash, no income. Allowed for financial and real instruments. The ledger code was already correct for it. | v1's retirement row moves pension rights from working-age members to pensioners, and homes must move with their owners as they age. Neither has a counterpart that issues the claim to route through. |
| 3 | compile.ts | Parameters that the closed-form `steadyState.solve` reads count as used (the compiler dry-runs `solve` on a recording record). | The data inputs of the closed form (population, employment rates, M3, tax revenues, ...) are read by nothing else, so each gave an "unused parameter" warning, although the warning text already says "or the steady-state solver". |
| 4 | harness `layers.ts` | Half-step sensitivity: the change may be 20% of the measure, or 10% of the width of the check's range when that is larger (only when both ends of the range are finite). | A measure near zero makes a relative change meaningless. v1's check "consumption at year 6 ÷ peak" is 0.02–0.04: the step length moves it by 0.01, which is 50% of the value but 5% of its range [0, 0.25]. v1 itself changes it by 33% when the step is halved. The tolerance widens only for measures smaller than half their range's width (4 Iceland checks, 1 reference check), and every other measure of both models still passes the old 20% test (the largest change is 4.8%). |

Tests: `tests/core/contract-0002.test.ts`.

## 3. How v1 maps onto the model language

- **Partial adjustment.** Every `s.x += kf(lam) (target − s.x)` of v1 is a rule with terms for the target and `adjust: { speed, form: 'exponential' }`, so the inspector shows the desired value and what moves it. Stock-gap *flows* (carry trade, portfolio rebalancing) use the same share per step as an annual rate (`gapRate` in `util.ts`).
- **Timing.** v1 computed a month in a fixed order, reading some states before and some after their update. Each read is now a same-step input or a lag, as v1 had it. Behavioural lags use `lastMonth()` (`util.ts`): the previous step at the standard step of a month, and still a month when the step is halved, so the model's reaction lags do not shrink with the step. Accounting lags (this month's price change for indexation and revaluation) stay one step.
- **One-offs.** A wage settlement and a króna sentiment shock set a "this month" state variable (`settlementJump`, `sentimentShock`) that is zero in every other month. As in v1, the settlement lands on top of the month's normal wage growth, and the sentiment shock on top of the faded sentiment. (Shocking the lagged wage directly would have let the error-correction term see the new wage one month early, 3% off v1.)
- **"After this month's flows".** v1 set firms' borrowing and bond issuance from deposits and the treasury account *after* the month's payments. Here they are identities of this month's cash gap (investment + tax + dividends − profit; the government's cash deficit) plus a top-up of any gap to target at 12 a year, which closes it within the month at the standard step: the same numbers as v1.
- **Portfolio rules** (bank bonds, pension-fund liquidity, older savers' bond share) use the start of the month's stocks, where v1 used stocks after the month's flows. The foreign-asset rule adds this month's revaluation, so funds react to a króna move in the same month, as in v1 (without it the króna path after a depreciation differed by 5%).
- **Pensions.** A payout is a redemption of pension rights: the fund pays cash and the pensioner's rights shrink. v1 posted a cash transfer and a separate accrual using up the rights, with the same stock changes. Retirement is a reclassification (change 2).
- **Money** is never computed: broad money is the sum of deposits, and every cash leg settles through the kernel's payment system. The same mortgage creates money when a bank lends and moves it when a pension fund does (module test), and deficits add more money when banks buy the bonds (module test and calibration check).
- **Indexation** of indexed mortgages and bonds is an `accrue` posting (module test: it moves no deposits).
- **Steady state.** `steady.ts` is v1's `40_steady.js`, extended with capital, homes and the new derived parameters. It returns every stock, every state variable and every solved or derived parameter, and the first step from it is an exact fixed point (residual 1.7e-13, no Newton iteration needed). Eleven balancing parameters are paired with the data they balance as the spec's free parameters and targets (tau0 ↔ government debt 56.7%, c0 ↔ each age group's deposits, muD ↔ non-residents' deposits, rho ↔ firms' debt, payout and ageing ↔ pension rights, mR ↔ mortgage debt), so the Newton polish keeps them for any variant.
- **Checks.** v1's 20 checks keep their scenarios and ranges; open ends are ±∞ instead of ±100. The bank-versus-pension-fund money check needs two scenarios, so its measure runs the pension-fund-financed counterpart itself on the same engine (`calibration.ts`).

## 5. Simplifications and improvements

Improvements over v1:
- Every flow is a set of legs with its own rule, so pipes are exact and each is explained; v1 split multi-party rows after the fact.
- Homes are a real asset: purchases between generations are trades, homes are revalued with house prices, and they move with their owners as they age, so households' net worth includes housing (v1: a memo item). The loan-to-value cap uses the value of the homes each group owns.
- Firms own their capital: investment buys machines and buildings, and depreciation (8% a year, assumed) writes them off, so firms' balance sheets are complete (v1 counted investment as current spending). Neither change moves any v1 result.
- Pension payouts follow the national-accounts treatment (a redemption of rights).
- Behavioural lags are a month whatever the step length (`lastMonth`), and partial adjustment is the exact first-order lag, so halving the step moves every calibration measure by at most 5% except the near-zero one above (v1's own half-step differences were up to 33%).
- Regimes name the binding constraint: debt-service cap, loan-to-value cap, stress-test floors, the zero lower bound, fixed key rate, capital premium, debt rule off.
- Every parameter has provenance: 61 values from `data/iceland/calibration.json` (or its notes) with source and vintage; 93 assumed, 30 placeholder and 19 tuned as in v1; and 71 solved or derived by the steady state, each saying what it is solved from.

Simplifications, kept from v1 or new:
- Shares are held at constant book value (as v1): owners' income arrives as dividends by fixed payout shares. The compiler notes that nothing posts to them.
- Public capital is not tracked: public investment is a spending channel, as in v1.
- Portfolio rebalancing (bank bonds, pension-fund liquidity, older savers' bonds) reacts to the start of the month's stocks (section 3).

## 6. Known gaps

- **Zero-growth, zero-inflation baseline**, as v1: pension payouts are about 14% of GDP against 6.3% in the data (balanced growth is v2).
- **The wage–price block and the nominal anchor (trade-nominal-drift, 29 September 2026).** The wage error correction pinned wages to domestic prices, `−phiW × log(w/pd)`, and hiring read the same `w/pd`. But domestic prices are a markup on a unit cost that is 45% imported inputs, so any lasting change in the real exchange rate (a terms-of-trade or export-demand shock) moved `w/pd` for good: the error correction never closed, and with half-anchored expectations (`chi` 0.5) that set off a wage–price spiral that never ended. In steady state inflation was about twice the error-correction term plus the Phillips term (tourism −15 on Manual: +1.05 pp at month 240, the price level +19.5% and the króna −22.5%, still falling). Now both measure wages against the *value-added price*, `pva = (pd − (1 − aLab) × deliveredImportPrice) ÷ aLab` (labour-and-wages.ts, `valueAddedPrice`): markup pricing keeps `w/pva` near its normal level whatever the króna does, as in the Scandinavian main-course model (Aukrust 1977), so a lasting real shock moves the price level once. The gap the error correction closes is the one bargainers see (`wageGapSeen`, following the actual gap at `lamWG`, 1 a year), and firms price a pay rise in faster than dearer imports (`lamUCw` 2.5 against `lamUC` 1.5); `phiW` 0.7 and `sigW` 0.3 were retuned so every wage-shock check stays in range (labour-LAB-2). Inflation at months 180/240, pp vs the no-change run, before → after: on Automatic tourism −15 +0.88/+0.82 → +0.00/+0.07, tourism +10 −0.53/−0.49 → −0.07/−0.09, foreign demand −20 +1.19/+1.17 → −0.03/+0.05, foreign demand +20 −1.15/−1.11 → −0.17/−0.19, fish prices +30 −1.88/−1.96 → −0.52/−0.50, fish −30 +2.15/+2.34 → +0.41/+0.45, aluminium +40 −0.22/−0.21 → −0.04/−0.04; on Manual (month 240) tourism −15 +1.05 → +0.46, foreign demand −20 +1.43 → +0.57, fish −30 +2.75 → +1.36, fish +30 −2.29 → −1.51.
  What remains, and who owns it:
  - *The fixed-`chi` slope.* Expectations stay a fixed mix of the target and remembered inflation, a documented anchored-expectations simplification, so the long-run Phillips curve has slope about `phiU ÷ chi`: a lasting unemployment gap leaves a lasting inflation gap rather than accelerating inflation. On Manual, with the key rate held, nothing else anchors inflation, and the Manual setting, the key-rate offset and the spending, tax and benefit definitions say so. The residual Manual trade gaps come from unemployment kept off its normal rate by a real exchange rate that keeps drifting (tourism −15: −0.31 pp at month 240).
  - *The external stock and portfolio loop and the rule (monetary-fx: FX-4, trade-taylor-fixed-potential).* The real exchange rate still drifts after month 240 under a lasting current-account change, the rule has a fixed neutral rate and fixed potential output, and its long-run response to steady inflation is `aPi × (1 − chi) + aPiA` = 0.95, below 1. Tourism +30 and foreign demand +20 on Automatic still leave about −0.3 and −0.2 pp; fish +30 still ends at the zero bound by month 600. Tourism −60 on Automatic, which before hid behind the spiral (+3.3 pp), now shows the rule's weakness the other way: the demand loss keeps unemployment 0.6 points up at month 240, the rule sits at its floor and inflation is −1.1 pp.
  - *The key-rate offset.* +1 pp on Automatic lowers inflation about 0.2 pp and output about 0.3% for good (−0.43% at month 240 for +1.25): partly a lower effective target, partly the stock-flow interest-income channel of the next item. Its definition says it is a departure from long-run neutrality.
- **Benefits, search and wage setting (labour-LAB-1, 29 September 2026).** The unemployment-benefit rate only scaled the benefit flow, so +30 points cut unemployment for good (−0.15 pp on Manual over months 60–240). More generous benefits now make people search longer (`benefitSearch`, `epsSearch` 0.25, building up at `lamSearch`), adding to the unemployed and the labour force but not restraining wages, and raise normal unemployment in wage setting by `uBenefit` (0.3 points at +30). +30 points: unemployment +0.61 pp (Manual) and +0.78 pp (Automatic) on average over months 60–240, the real wage within 0.5% of baseline.
- **Firms' debt and payout (trade-exporter-debt-spiral, trade-fish-windfall-hoarded, 29 September 2026).** Decision 0003 records the new payout rule. Fisheries' loans under fish prices −30 held 50 years: 78% of GDP (Manual) and 349% (Automatic) by month 600 → below 11% (1.7 times their normal share); tourism +30 on Automatic: 307% → 1.65 times. Fish +30 over five years: dividends 0.70 → 3.0% of GDP-years, loans repaid 5.6 → 1.4, fishing fee 1.4 (new). The fall in output and the rise in unemployment after a fish windfall are the króna's lasting appreciation (the external loop above), not the payout.
- **Households' cash buffer (tax-TAX-2, 29 September 2026).** Households now cut spending as their savings fall below five months of it (`aBuf`, `bufMonths`), and the cash limit is a backstop. Income tax +5 held on Manual: working-age deposits 0.31 of 13.8 left at month 240 → 4.5; VAT +10: empty from month 150 → 4.4; the cash limit no longer binds in either. The late collapse of income tax +10 is the portfolio-balance term (tax-TAX-1, monetary-fx).
- **Budgets are sticky in krónur (tax-TAX-7).** The consumption habit acts on spending in krónur, so a rise in prices cuts what households buy at once (VAT +2.5: real consumption −0.74% in month 1, the whole price rise), while an income-tax rise of the same size works through gradually (−0.20%). A habit on real spending removed the difference (−0.05% for VAT) but raised the wage-shock key-rate peak to about 1.8–2.0 points, above DYNIMO's 1–1.5, and the model's wage calibration could not bring it back with any combination tried, so it is kept and stated in the consumption rule.
- **Floors in extreme settings (audit H1, H2, H6, H7, M5, M6, M9–M11, M22).** Balance sheets must stay possible: no holder's asset below zero, no issuer's liability turned into an asset, no negative real capital, and no negative unemployment. The accounting checks cannot see this, so each channel that broke it has a floor, written as a `combine` of the existing rule with a plain-English regime. None binds at the baseline (drift stays below 1e-9). Two bind inside calibration scenarios, and the calibration results include them: the deposit-rate floor in the tourism slump (from month 6) and non-residents' bond sales to keep króna cash in the króna depreciation (from month 18). The harness now fails on any breach (decision 0005): the property runs, the sweep of every lever alone at its min and at its max for 240 months in each mode, and the golden scenarios must show no wrong-signed position that is not declared exempt, and no unemployment rate outside [0, 50%], unemployed pool below zero, price index at or below zero or negative key rate.
  - *Firms:* gross investment is never negative ("No new investment: capital only wears out"), so capital only depreciates; net borrowing is at least −loans ÷ one step ("Loans repaid in full: spare cash stays in deposits").
  - *Government bonds:* a buyback never exceeds the nominal bonds banks, the central bank, pension funds and older households hold, and is taken from them in proportion to their holdings whatever the bond-buyer choice; the rest of a surplus stays in the treasury account ("Buyback limited by holdings"). Pension funds and older households chosen as buyers take only what their deposits pay for; banks take the rest. The debt ratio nets treasury cash above its target.
  - *Trades with banks:* pension funds, older households and non-residents sell at most the bonds they hold after the month's buyback, and buy at most what banks still hold (in that order).
  - *Liquidity:* nobody pays with deposits they do not have. A payer may draw at most 1 − e^(−`liquiditySpeed` × one month) (63%) of its deposits in a month beyond its income. Pension funds and non-residents keep a cash buffer, `pfLiquidityFloorShare` and `wDepositFloorShare` (half) of their usual deposit share, and draw 63% a month of what is above it; below it the same limit turns negative and becomes the cash they must raise, 63% of the shortfall a month. One smooth limit for buying and selling replaced a switch that sold the whole shortfall at once and then bought with 63% of all deposits: that switch took them back below the buffer the next month, and levers inside their ranges gave sawtooth paths (review E1: foreign allocation +20 on Automatic reversed the funds' foreign purchases 97 times in ten years, a króna shock of −25% reversed non-residents' bond trades 96 times; now 0 and at most 2, and no chart or floor-driven flow reverses more than 10 times in 20 years under any lever alone at its min or max, `tests/models/iceland-smooth-floors.test.ts`). Pension funds spend the limit in turn on new bonds, foreign assets, bank bonds and bonds from banks (new bonds too are paid only from deposits above the buffer, and banks take the rest: before, new bonds could take 63% of all their deposits, and a deficit they bought ran the buffer down every month and forced foreign sales to rebuild it; with pension funds as bond buyers and the income-tax offset at −10 on Automatic that lifted the króna 17.5% by month 34, and now it is never stronger than at the start); below the buffer they sell foreign assets, but never more than non-residents have krónur for, and let bank bonds run off for whatever foreign sales cannot raise; the foreign-allocation lever shrinks their bank-bond and deposit targets in proportion. Non-residents sell bonds below theirs, and always at least enough to keep their deposits above zero. Households spend at most their cash income plus that share of their deposits (older households keep `hoBondCashShare`, half of it, for bonds). The deposit rate is never below 0%. `liquiditySpeed` and the three shares are assumed parameters in `params.ts`.
  - *Unemployment (M5, M22):* when job gains would take an age group's unemployed below `uFloorStart` of their normal number, the migration share rises smoothly, so more of the extra jobs go to people arriving from abroad, and the pool approaches `uFloor` of normal (people between jobs) without ever reaching zero ("Few unemployed left: extra jobs go to people arriving from abroad"). Unemployment benefits therefore never turn negative.
  - *Banks' reserves (declared exempt, decision 0005):* once a surplus held on Manual has bought back every bond it can (income tax +10 held for more than about 13 years; public investment −3 held for 18 years did too, until firms' debt was held near its norm and households kept a cash buffer), the treasury account keeps the rest and taxes paid into it drain banks' reserves one for one, below zero. This is not floored: a negative reserve balance is the banks borrowing reserves from the central bank's lending facility, charged at the key rate, which is what a central bank does when a treasury surplus drains the system. `reserves` declares `mayGoNegative` for `B` and `CB` with that reason; it is the model's only exemption. On Automatic the debt rule cuts taxes first.

  The model test `tests/models/iceland-balance-sheets.test.ts` runs every lever alone at its min and at its max for 20 years in each mode where it acts, and the worst combinations; before the floors, 59 positions in 35 of those runs had the wrong sign, and after them none does (the reserves above, exempt, appear in exactly the one run named). In 450 random two- and three-lever combinations at their extremes (both modes), 534 of 900 runs had a wrong-sign position other than reserves before the floors, and 2 after. What remains is reached only by combining several levers at their extremes, which the harness does not do (each pinned by the model test):
  - **Non-residents' krónur under a large current-account surplus (closed, review M6).** Exporters are paid from non-residents' króna deposits. When those deposits and all their government bonds were used up (pension funds bringing 20 points of assets home while tourism is 30% up on Automatic, or a long Manual surplus with strong foreign demand), the surplus overdrew their deposits, by up to 0.02 and 0.11% of GDP. Non-residents now borrow krónur from Icelandic banks (`kronaLoansW`, W the issuer, banks the holder; zero at baseline): exactly what a month's payments and bond trade would overdraw, at the key rate, repaid from deposits above the share they keep at the liquidity speed. The loan creates their deposit as any bank loan does; its interest is Iceland's income in the current account and the banks' profit. In those two cases they borrow at most 0.43% of GDP (months 10–33) and 0.26% (months 114–148) and repay it; the rule's regime names each month they borrow or repay. Their gross króna holdings can still approach zero, where the portfolio-balance term's floor (5% of normal holdings) stops the króna strengthening further.
  - **Pension funds in a collapse.** If the economy collapses for 20 years (public investment −3, foreign demand +20 and income tax +10 held on Manual), contributions fall toward zero while pensions are paid in full. The funds sell their government bonds, foreign assets and bank bonds, then overdraw deposits by up to 2.4% of GDP, because they never sell shares or mortgages and pensions are never cut. VAT +10 with income tax +10 held on Manual reached the same point in the last months of the 20 years until households kept a cash buffer and firms' owners held their debt near its norm; it no longer does.
  - **No shutdown.** At the lowest aluminium prices the smelters make losses for years and their foreign parents keep them going with negative dividends; they stop investing but cannot cut output or close.
  - **No unsecured credit.** A household whose deposits run out spends only its income; it cannot borrow except through mortgages.
- **Foreign interest rate (review E3).** A higher foreign rate used to strengthen the króna a great deal (+5 pp: 13% stronger after a year, 72% after ten on Manual, and on Automatic a decade at the zero floor with inflation 3 pp below baseline). The pension funds' foreign income rose by the full 5 points on all of their foreign assets (75% of GDP), was converted into krónur from non-residents' deposits (7% of GDP), and the portfolio-balance term turned that drain into appreciation within three months, far outweighing the carry trade. Two channels changed: only the funds' foreign bonds and deposits (`pfForeignRatePass`, 30%) earn more when rates abroad rise, not their shares; and the funds raise their foreign target by `psiPF` (1) point of assets per point of foreign rate, as uncovered interest parity says domestic savers do. Now +1 pp weakens the króna by about 0.9% on average over two years (calibration check `foreign-rate-krona-2y`), and +5 pp by about 5% in year 1; the key rate rises about 1 pp instead of hitting the floor. What remains is a known gap: the extra foreign income, spent at home, strengthens the króna from about month 40 (+5 pp: 12% stronger after ten years on Automatic, 17% on Manual; before, 55% and 72%), and prices drift down, because the model has no steady state with a lasting surplus of foreign income: non-residents' krónur keep draining while falling prices hold their real value up. The drift does not level off. After twenty years, per point held, the króna is about 3.5–4% stronger and the price level about 1.2% lower on Automatic (8–10.5% and 5% on Manual), with inflation still below baseline (+5 pp on Automatic: 19% stronger, prices 6% lower, inflation 0.5 pp below; before the value-added price, 5–6% and 3% per point, 29% and 15% at +5; `tests/models/iceland-krona.test.ts` pins the per-point values and the lever definition says the same). Fixing it is the nominal-drift design decision that owns every lasting current-account change (fish prices +30 and the foreign allocation drift the same way): for example scaling the portfolio-balance term by nominal holdings, or a steady state with a nonzero current account. The key rate's effect on the funds' allocation is left out: most of their domestic assets are indexed at fixed real rates, and adding it pushed the key-rate calibration checks outside their bands.
- **A high key rate held for years on Manual turns expansionary (review E7).** At 15% held on Manual, output falls about 6% in the first two years, but is back above baseline from month 83 and about 5.6% above at month 120 (month 91 before wages were measured against the value-added price), while government debt rises by about 158 points of GDP. With taxes and spending held too, the government pays 15% on a debt that grows with its own interest, and that interest is income for households and pension funds (their property income is about twice its baseline by year 10), which they spend. This is the interest-income channel of stock-flow-consistent models (Godley and Lavoie 2007, *Monetary Economics*, model PC: a higher interest rate raises steady-state income when the government does not respond), not an error, so it is kept and explained in the key-rate lever's definition. With the debt rule acting (Automatic, a +5-point offset to the rule for 20 years), output stays 1.6–1.8% below baseline throughout; a model test pins both.
- **Exchange-rate pass-through (review E6).** Import prices in krónur followed world prices × the exchange rate one for one within about two years, and both the CPI's imported goods and firms' imported inputs (45% of unit cost) were priced at them, so world prices +10% raised the CPI 2.7% in a year and 3.7% in two, against the 1.5–2.3% of CBI WP85. Buyers in Iceland now pay a delivered price, (1 − `distM`) × import prices + `distM` × domestic prices, with `distM` 0.35: the Icelandic cost of getting the goods to them (Campa and Goldberg 2010 find distribution margins of 30–50% of purchasers' prices for consumer goods; one margin also serves firms' inputs, which carry less). Imports are still paid for at import prices, so what Iceland pays abroad still moves one for one with world prices in krónur. World prices +10% now raise the CPI 1.98% in a year and 2.81% in two, and the check `world-prices-cpi-year1` uses the cited range 1.5–2.3. A króna held 10% weaker passes 0.23 per point through within a year, and the check `krona-pass-through-year1` (price level at month 12 ÷ the average depreciation, 0.218 in WP85's 0.15–0.23) replaces v1's `krona-price-level-8q`, whose band at month 24 asked for about twice WP85's pass-through. What remains: within the quarter the model passes through 0.07, not 0.15 (import prices follow at `lamPm`, 2 a year), and beyond the first year it rises a little as wages catch up and then levels off, to 0.30 after two years and 0.32 after three, between WP85's long-run 0.23 and the IMF's 0.4 at 36 months (before the value-added price, 0.34 and 0.43 and still rising: a lasting depreciation set off a wage–price spiral). As a side effect, wages now reach prices a little more, and `wage-unemployment-peak` (0.515) moved inside its cited 0.5–1, which is now its range.
- **Calibration margins (review E5).** No range was widened. These results sit near an edge and are calibration work, not passes to lean on: rate-output-timing 4 and rate-inflation-timing 5 (on the lower bound, the documented takeover artefact), rate-inflation-trough −0.333 against −0.35 (a known gap until 29 September 2026: now −0.311, and −0.34 with a gradual takeover), wage-unemployment-peak 0.515 against 0.5 (the cited range since review E6; 0.888 since 29 September 2026), krona-pass-through-year1 0.218 against 0.23 (0.214), rate-krona 0.415 against 0.3, wage-key-rate-peak 1.462 against 1.5 (since 29 September 2026), and wage-back-consumption 0.198 against 0.25 (0.205) (its half-step change, 0.198 to 0.208, is half the limit; before review E6 it was 0.001, a near-zero ratio that passed the half-step test only through the harness's band fallback). Judging near-zero ratios on an absolute band and flagging results within 5% of an edge are harness changes, left to its owner.
- A key rate held at 15% on Manual for 30 years stays finite (an inflationary spiral: nominal consumption is four times its baseline by then).
- **Newton cannot find the baseline alone.** The steady state has directions that nothing pins down: the price level (a zero-inflation model with no price-level target), holdings nobody trades at baseline (the central bank's bonds, shares) and older households' homes. The closed form sets them; Newton only polishes (it needs no iteration at the default parameters), and from a perturbed guess it converges slowly within its 60 iterations.
- **Placeholders** remain where v1 had them (30 values, flagged; v1's hard-coded 0.15 older households' weight in exporters' dividends is now one of them): firm investment and loans, the central bank's balance sheet, the import share of public purchases, housing values and turnover, pension-fund liquidity and surplus, and several payout shares.
